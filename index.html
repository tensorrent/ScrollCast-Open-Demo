The problem

In the theatre you would never mail an unlocked master.

Cinemas solved this decades ago. A DCP ships encrypted and the key — the KDM — is issued to one server, for one window of dates. When the run ends, the key stops working. Nobody thinks that is unreasonable.

Online delivery skipped that step. A Drive link, a WeTransfer, a password-protected Vimeo page, a downloaded screener: each one is a copy sitting on a machine you do not control. You can change the password. You cannot change the copy.

Sending a file

You send the thing itself

  • Every recipient now holds a complete, playable master.
  • Forwarding is invisible to you and costs them nothing.
  • Revoking a link does not reach a download.
  • You cannot tell a second viewing from a second viewer.
Sending a ticket

You send permission

  • The package travels sealed; the key never rides with it.
  • Access is a ticket: N plays, until a date, or until you cancel.
  • Cancelling stops the next redemption everywhere at once.
  • The link is safe to post — without a ticket it plays nothing.

How it works

Four steps. The film and the permission travel apart.

That separation is the whole idea. Anyone can hold the package; only a live ticket turns it back into a picture.

01

Seal

Your master is packaged into ordinary streaming segments, encrypted, and signed with your publisher key. The key is held back — it is not in the package.

02

Send

The recipient gets a link. On its own it plays nothing, so it is safe in an email, a submission portal, or a public post.

03

Admit

You issue a ticket governed by a rule — three plays, ten days, or open until you say otherwise. The server releases the key one session at a time.

04

Verify

Every segment is re-addressed from the bytes that arrived and checked against your signature before the decoder is allowed to touch it.

Limits

What this does not do.

Worth saying before anyone asks. A security claim is only useful if its edges are stated.

It does not stop a camera pointed at a screen.

Nothing does. Tickets govern the next redemption; they cannot un-see a play that already happened.

It is not Widevine or FairPlay.

Once a play is granted, that browser held a key. This is the same ceiling every clear-key scheme has. What it adds is that access is countable, expirable, and cancellable — and that integrity is enforced before decode.

It does not watermark or trace individual viewers.

There is no forensic watermarking here and none is claimed. What is recorded is redemption: which ticket was used, when, and how many plays remain.

It is not a compression codec.

The picture stays H.264/AAC in ordinary CMAF segments. This wraps the codec; it does not compete with it.

It does not replace a DCP in the booth.

Theatrical projection has its own chain and should keep it. This is for everything that currently travels as a link.
